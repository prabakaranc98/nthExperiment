# Writing and Thinking

> I want to become a writer — not as a career, but as a thinking instrument. Writing that is clear, precise, and alive. Writing that forces the thinking to sharpen, not just the prose.

---

## The Premise

Writing is not the output of thinking. It is thinking made visible — and forced to be honest. You cannot write clearly about something you do not understand. The sentence that falls apart is telling you something. The paragraph that loops back on itself is telling you something. The essay that loses the reader halfway through is telling you something — about the structure of the idea, not just the structure of the prose.

The polymaths who lasted — Montaigne, Darwin, Feynman, Hofstadter — were all extraordinary writers. Not because they were stylists but because they *thought through language*. Writing was their laboratory. The essay was their experiment.

This subject is about developing that. Not beautiful sentences for their own sake. Clear sentences because clarity is precision, and precision is the thing.

---

## Core Questions
- What makes an idea legible to a reader who doesn't already believe it?
- How do you write about complex technical ideas without losing the non-specialist, without losing the specialist, and without lying?
- What is the relationship between sentence structure and thought structure?
- How do you develop a genuine voice — one that is yours, not a composite of influences?
- What is the essay as a form, and why does it still work?

---

## The Two Practices

**1. Read like a writer**
Read the writers below not for what they say but for how they do it. Ask: what is the structure of this argument? Where does the energy come from? How does the sentence move? What would this look like if it were badly written?

**2. Write to think**
Every experiment in this curriculum produces writing. Some of it should be aimed at a reader — a distillation, an essay, an explanation. Writing for a reader forces you to know what you actually think. The test: can you explain this to someone who is smart but has no context, without condescending and without oversimplifying?

## Essential Starting Points

- [ ] **Politics and the English Language — George Orwell**
  - Full essay — the six rules, the politics of vague language
  - Read it twice: once for the argument, once for how he does what he's saying

- [ ] **The Elements of Style — Strunk & White**
  - Part II: Elementary Principles of Composition (all)
  - Part V: An Approach to Style (rules 1–8, 16–21)

- [ ] **On Writing Well — William Zinsser**
  - Ch. 2: Simplicity
  - Ch. 3: Clutter
  - Ch. 5: The Audience
  - Ch. 16: Science and Technology *(most relevant)*

- [ ] **The Sense of Style — Steven Pinker**
  - Ch. 2: A Window Into the World (classic style as the ideal)
  - Ch. 3: The Curse of Knowledge (why experts write badly)
  - Ch. 4: The Web, the Tree, and the String (sentence structure as thought structure)

- [ ] **Consider the Lobster — David Foster Wallace** *(essays)*
  - "Authority and American Usage" — on precision, care, and what it costs
  - "Consider the Lobster" — how to write about a complex thing with real stakes

- [ ] **Surely You're Joking, Mr. Feynman — Feynman**
  - Not a writing book — but study *how* he explains things. The voice, the concreteness, the refusal to hide behind jargon.

- [ ] **The Complete Essays — Montaigne** *(selected)*
  - "To Philosophize is to Learn to Die"
  - "On Experience"
  - "On the Education of Children"
  - *(The inventor of the essay — read to understand what the form can do)*

## Writing on AI, Intelligence and Consciousness

A special focus: writing clearly about ideas that are genuinely hard. The philosophy of mind, the nature of intelligence, the hard problem of consciousness — these are domains where bad writing is epidemic, where jargon hides confusion, and where the writer who can make the difficulty *felt* without falsifying it is rare.

**Writers who do this well — study them:**
- [ ] **Douglas Hofstadter** — *Gödel, Escher, Bach*, *I Am a Strange Loop* — how to write about recursion, consciousness, and self-reference without losing the reader or the idea
- [ ] **Daniel Dennett** — *Consciousness Explained*, *Darwin's Dangerous Idea* — polemic philosophical writing, clear and combative
- [ ] **David Chalmers** — *The Conscious Mind* — how to write about the hard problem with philosophical rigor
- [ ] **Melanie Mitchell** — *Artificial Intelligence: A Guide for Thinking Humans* — technical ideas for an intelligent general reader
- [ ] **John Searle** — "Minds, Brains, and Programs" *(the Chinese Room paper)* — how a short paper can shift a whole debate

## Writing Experiments to Run

- **The Reconstruction Essay:** Pick a paper you've read. Explain the core argument in under 500 words for someone who hasn't read it. No jargon without definition. No appeals to authority.
- **The Steel-Man:** Take a position you disagree with in AI or philosophy of mind. Write the best possible version of the argument — better than the original if you can.
- **The Technical-to-Human Translation:** Take something from your experiments (a result, an architecture, an insight) and write about it for a smart non-technical reader.
- **The Essay-as-Experiment:** Write an essay that *argues* something — a specific claim about AI, intelligence, or decision-making — and treat writing it as testing whether the claim holds.
