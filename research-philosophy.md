# How to Do Research
### *On building a genuine research craft — and the one thing that destroys it*

---

## The one thing that destroys it

Before anything else: **performative research** — work done to signal relevance rather than understand something — is the single most corrosive force in a research career. It looks like productivity. It produces papers. It gets cited. And it leaves you, five years later, with a list of publications and no actual understanding of anything.

The signs:
- Picking a problem because it's trending, not because it's important or confusing to you
- Publishing a result before you understand *why* it's true
- Pivoting to the newest model or benchmark to stay "relevant"
- Measuring your work by attention, not by whether it changed your understanding or the field's
- Knowing the answer before you do the work

This document is about the alternative. The people below — Feynman, Hamming, and the researchers who built the AI frontier — all rejected performative research explicitly and paid the short-term cost. The long-term compounding was extraordinary.

---

## Feynman — Curiosity as the Only Compass

Richard Feynman's research career was driven by one principle: *work on things that genuinely interest you, and ignore everything else*.

> "I don't know what's the matter with people: they don't learn by understanding, they learn by some other way — by rote or something. Their knowledge is so fragile."

> "Fall in love with some activity, and do it! Nobody ever figures out what life is all about, and it doesn't matter. Explore the world. Nearly everything is really interesting if you go into it deeply enough."

> "Study hard what interests you the most in the most undisciplined, irreverent and original manner possible."

**What Feynman means for your research:**

1. **"What I cannot create, I do not understand."** This is the standard. Not "I read the paper." Not "I can explain it." Can you derive it from first principles? Can you build it? If not, you understand a description, not the thing itself. The arcs and capstones exist for exactly this reason.

2. **The pleasure principle.** Feynman famously hit a wall at Cornell — paralyzed by the weight of expectation after the Manhattan Project. He recovered by deciding he would only work on things that were *fun*, that gave him genuine pleasure to think about. The great results (QED, the superfluidity work) came from this play. If your research feels like performance, it is.

3. **Curiosity about the mechanism, not the result.** Feynman was famous for caring about *why* something worked. The result was almost secondary. "The Pleasure of Finding Things Out" — not the pleasure of having found something that would get cited.

**The anti-Feynman move** (what to avoid): picking a research question by asking "what would be impressive to publish?" instead of "what am I actually confused about and want to understand?"

## Hamming — Working on Important Problems

Richard Hamming spent decades at Bell Labs and gave one of the most important lectures ever delivered to scientists: "You and Your Research" (1986). It is available free online. Read it once a year.

> "If you do not work on an important problem, it's unlikely you'll do important work."

> "Most scientists spend their time working on problems that are not important, and often they know it, but they say they don't know what an important problem is — and yet they do know, they just won't face up to it."

> "What are the most important problems in your field? And why aren't you working on them?"

**The Hamming questions — ask them regularly:**

1. *What are the most important open problems in your area?* (Be specific. Vague answers don't count.)
2. *What would it mean to solve one of them?* What would change if you did?
3. *What is the most important thing you could be working on right now — and why aren't you working on it?*
4. *What are the important problems adjacent to what you're currently doing?*

**The Friday afternoon sessions.** Hamming reserved Friday afternoons to think speculatively — about big problems, about what mattered, about connections between fields. He credited this practice as the source of most of his best ideas. It was unscheduled, unjustified, unproductive by any short-term metric. It was the most important time of his week.

**On courage.** Hamming's most underappreciated point: *research requires courage*. Courage to work on problems that might not pan out. Courage to say "I don't know" and mean it. Courage to pursue an idea the field isn't interested in yet. Courage to spend a year on something that turns out to be wrong — and treat that as progress.

> "The great scientists, when an opportunity opens up, get after it and they pursue it. They drop all other things. They get rid of other things and they get after an idea because they had already thought the thing through. Their minds are prepared; they see the opportunity and they go after it."

**On knowledge and open doors.** Hamming observed that the most productive scientists had *open doors* — they talked to people, stayed aware of problems in adjacent areas, let ideas cross-pollinate. The scientists with closed doors did good work. The scientists with open doors did great work.

**The anti-Hamming move**: spending your research career on "safe" problems — incremental, publishable, unlikely to fail — while knowing that the important problems sit nearby and you're avoiding them because they're hard and might not pan out.

## Contemporary AI Leaders — The Craft at the Frontier

### Andrej Karpathy — Build to understand

> "The most effective way to understand something deeply is to implement it from scratch."

Karpathy's public research style is the Feynman principle applied to deep learning: build `micrograd`, then `minGPT`, then `nanoGPT` — not because these are novel, but because building them forces understanding that reading never does. The famous "A Recipe for Training Neural Networks" is not about tricks — it's about developing *taste* through building.

**What this means for FAIRE:** The arcs and capstones are not portfolio projects. They are the understanding. The code is the thinking.

> "Don't read a paper and feel satisfied. Read a paper and then do the work that would make you believe it or not believe it."

### Chris Olah — Research debt and genuine understanding

Olah coined the term *research debt* — the gap between what the field knows and what it understands. Papers communicate results; they don't always communicate understanding.

> "Research debt is the accumulation of missing interpretive frameworks, intuitions, and explanations. It's the reason a field can advance rapidly on benchmarks while the researchers in it can't explain to each other why their methods work."

His response was *distillation* — not writing more papers, but building the understanding that makes the field's results legible. This is what the `lecture-myself/` folder and `library/` are for.

**The anti-Olah move**: consuming papers without ever building the understanding that lets you explain them. Citation is not comprehension.

### Ilya Sutskever — Think for long periods about hard problems

> "The biggest thing is to have this ability to sit and think for a long time. Researchers who are willing to think really hard for a year or two on something — and most people aren't — can make real progress."

Sutskever's advice is about depth, not breadth. The AI field moves fast. The temptation is to chase every new result. The researchers who make lasting contributions sit with hard problems long enough to actually understand them.

**The anti-Sutskever move**: reading every new paper, tweeting about every new benchmark, staying informed about everything while understanding nothing deeply.

### Yoshua Bengio — Long-termism against the tide

Bengio worked on deep learning for a decade during the "AI winter" when the field thought neural networks were dead. He did it because he believed the ideas were right, not because they were popular.

> "Don't follow the herd. Work on what you believe is important, even if it isn't fashionable."

This is the most direct counter to performative research: your research agenda should be driven by what you think matters, not by what is currently getting citations or attention.

### Geoffrey Hinton — Conviction over consensus

Hinton's career is a case study in sustained contrarianism. He was wrong many times. He was also right about deep learning when almost everyone else was convinced it was wrong. The lesson is not "be contrarian" — it is: *let your conviction come from your own thinking and your own experimental results, not from what the community currently believes*.

## The Synthesis — What Genuine Research Looks Like

Drawing from all of the above, the profile of genuine research work:

**It starts with confusion, not a gap in the literature.**
You are confused about something. You can't explain it. The thing that confuses you — genuinely, persistently — is the seed of a research question. A gap in the literature that you notice but don't actually care about is not a research question; it's a positioning move.

**It is question-driven, not answer-driven.**
You commit to the question before you know the answer. If you already know the answer when you start the work, you're writing down something you already know. That's useful, but it's not research.

**It goes deep enough to break your prior understanding.**
The goal of a research project is not to confirm what you thought. It's to find the place where your understanding breaks — and fix it. If you end a project with the same mental model you started with, you didn't go deep enough.

**It produces something genuinely reusable.**
A cleaned-up implementation. A mental model that survives contact with new examples. A method that works in a different domain than you tested it in. A negative result that saves the next person a year. "Reusable" means someone else (or future-you) can actually use it — not just read about it.

**It is honest about what it doesn't know.**
The most important sentences in any research write-up are the ones that say: "this is where our analysis breaks down," "we don't know why this works," "this result doesn't hold under these conditions." Hiding the limitations is the most destructive form of performative research.

## The Anti-Patterns (what this program is explicitly not)

| Performative research | Genuine research |
|----------------------|-----------------|
| Pick the problem that will look good on a CV | Pick the problem you're actually confused by |
| Chase whatever is trending in the field | Work on what you think is important |
| Publish incremental work fast to stay visible | Go deep until you actually understand |
| Optimize for benchmark numbers | Optimize for understanding the mechanism |
| Know the answer before you do the work | Commit to the question before you know the answer |
| Measure success by citations and attention | Measure success by change in understanding |
| Stay relevant by pivoting to new methods | Stay relevant by understanding things more deeply |
| Write about building things | Build things |

---

## The Practice

Three concrete habits that protect against performative drift:

**1. The Hamming question, monthly.**
At the start of each month: what are the three most important open problems in what I'm studying? Am I working on any of them? If not, why not — and is the reason good?

**2. The Feynman test, per paper.**
After reading any paper: can I derive the core result from scratch? Can I build the minimal version that demonstrates the claim? If not, reading is not done.

**3. The Olah journal, per arc.**
After each build: what is the precise thing I now understand that I didn't before? Write it in one sentence. If you can't write that sentence, the arc didn't produce understanding — it produced code.

*"The work is to understand. Everything else — the papers, the code, the talks, the citations — is evidence that you understood."*
