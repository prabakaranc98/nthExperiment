# Lens B — Hamming + Scale

**Type:** Lens
**Serves:** Sharpens taste, problem selection, and the "important problems" filter — the meta-skill of knowing what's worth working on.

---

## Why This Lens

Most researchers are good at solving problems. Very few are good at choosing them. Hamming spent his career asking: "What are the most important problems in your field — and why aren't you working on them?" Scale gives the other half: the empirical patterns of how cities, companies, organisms, and civilizations grow, plateau, and collapse. Together these form a lens for asking: *is what I'm working on actually important, and what does important even mean at scale?*

This is a career-shaping lens, not just an intellectual one.

---

## What "From Scratch" Means Here
- No frameworks borrowed uncritically. Test every claim against your own experience and observation.
- Build a personal "important problems" list. Update it every month. Track how it changes.
- Apply the scale lens to AI: what are the actual scaling laws, where do they break, what do they imply about what to build?

## Core Ideas

### Hamming's Filter
From his Friday afternoon sessions at Bell Labs: "What are the most important problems in your field? What would it take to work on one of them?" Most people have answers to the first but not the second. The second is where the work is.

**The practice:** Every month, write the three most important open problems in each subject you're studying. Be specific. Then ask: what would I need to know or build to make progress on any of them?

### West's Scale
From Geoffrey West's *Scale*: living things, cities, and companies obey predictable power laws. Metabolism scales as the ¾ power of mass. Cities scale super-linearly (double the population, get more than double the patents, wages, crime). Companies scale sub-linearly — and eventually die. What do these patterns tell us about AI scaling?

**The claim to test:** Do the scaling laws for LLMs (Kaplan et al.) fit the West pattern, or are they a different kind of scaling? What breaks first?

### Carlota Perez — Technological Revolutions
Technology revolutions follow a recurring pattern: installation period (infrastructure + speculation + crash) → turning point → deployment period (diffusion + real productivity). We are somewhere in the AI installation period. Where exactly?

### Tainter — Collapse
Complexity is a problem-solving strategy that has diminishing returns. Societies collapse when the marginal return on complexity investment falls below the cost of sustaining it. The question for AI: are we building systems that solve the complexity problem or systems that compound it?

## Build Progression

- [ ] **Step 1:** Read Hamming's "You and Your Research" (essay). Write your own list: what are the three most important problems in AI right now, and what would it take to work on one of them?
- [ ] **Step 2:** Read *Scale* (West) — the power law chapters. Plot the AI scaling law curves (Kaplan) alongside biological scaling curves. What's the same? What's different?
- [ ] **Step 3:** Read Perez (selected). Map the current AI moment onto her framework. Which phase are we in and what does that predict?
- [ ] **Step 4:** Read Tainter (selected). Apply the marginal return analysis to one AI research program you're familiar with.
- [ ] **Step 5:** Write: "What Hamming would ask about current AI research" — a short, sharp essay.
